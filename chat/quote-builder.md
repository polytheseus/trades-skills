# Quotes and estimates for trades work

Most disputes in trades work start with a vague quote: "Fit new bathroom,
£6,500." The customer remembers what they imagined; the trader remembers
what they priced. A good written quote says exactly what's included, what
isn't, what was assumed, and what happens if something changes. It also wins
more work, because it looks organised next to a price scribbled on a
WhatsApp message.

This skill writes the document. **The trader sets the price.** Never invent
rates, material costs or labour times. If they're missing, ask, or leave a
clearly marked `[£ ?]`.

## Step 1: Gather the facts

Read whatever the trader gives you (notes, a voice-note transcript, photos
described in words, the customer's message). Ask only for what's missing,
in one short list:

1. **Trade and job**: what's being done, where (room/area), and roughly how
   big (sizes, quantities, number of points/units).
2. **Price**: a fixed total, or labour + materials, or a day rate. Is VAT /
   sales tax included, extra, or not applicable (e.g. not VAT-registered)?
3. **Customer type**: a homeowner (consumer) or a business. This changes
   cancellation rights and payment terms (see Step 4).
4. **Who supplies what**: does the trader supply materials, or the
   customer (e.g. tiles, sanitaryware, light fittings)?
5. **Timing**: start date or lead time, and expected duration.
6. **Deposit and payment stages** the trader wants.
7. **Location**: UK, US (which state) or elsewhere, for spelling, tax
   wording and the legal notes.

If the trader says "just do it", draft from what you have and mark every gap.

## Step 2: Quote or estimate?

Make the trader choose, and label the document accordingly:

- **Quote (fixed price)**: the price is binding for the scope described. Use
  it when the job is well defined.
- **Estimate**: a best guess that can change. Use it when you can't see
  what's behind walls, under floors or in the roof. Give a range or a
  ceiling ("estimated £1,800–£2,300; we'll call you before exceeding
  £2,300") and say what would push it up.

In the UK, customers and courts tend to treat a "quote" as a fixed price,
so don't call something a quote if it isn't. Many US states regulate
estimates for home improvement (e.g. written estimates, limits on overruns
without consent). Tell US traders to check their state's home improvement
rules and their licensing board.

## Step 3: Write the document

Use this structure. Keep it to one or two pages.

```
[Business name] · [address/phone/email] · [registration / licence / VAT no. if any]

QUOTE  (or ESTIMATE)    No. [Q-0001]    Date: [date]    Valid until: [date, default 30 days]

For: [Customer name]    Job address: [address]

1. The job (summary)
   One or two sentences in plain English: what the customer will end up with.

2. Scope of work: what's included
   Numbered, specific lines. Quantities, sizes, brands/specs where it matters.
   Include prep, protection, making good, waste removal and clean-up if included.

3. Not included (exclusions)
   Everything the customer might assume is included but isn't.

4. Assumptions
   What the price relies on (e.g. "existing pipework is 15 mm copper in good
   condition", "clear access to the loft", "parking available on the drive").

5. Price
   Itemised table: Labour | Materials | Other (skip/disposal, hire, permits) |
   Subtotal | VAT/tax (rate) | TOTAL. Say clearly whether VAT/tax is included.
   Optional extras as separate priced lines the customer can tick.

6. Timing
   Earliest start, expected duration, what could delay it (weather, lead times).

7. Payment terms
   Deposit (amount, what it's for, when it's due), stage payments tied to
   visible milestones, final balance due on completion within [X] days,
   accepted payment methods.

8. Changes and extra work
   "Any change to the scope will be agreed in writing, with a price, before
   the work is done."

9. Guarantee / warranty
   What the trader guarantees and for how long; manufacturer warranties
   passed on; what isn't covered (misuse, customer-supplied parts).

10. How to accept
    "To go ahead, reply to this email/message saying you accept quote
    [Q-0001], or sign below." Plus a signature/date line.
```

### Scope and exclusions: the bit that saves arguments

Write scope lines that a stranger could check off. "Supply and fit 8 new
LED downlights (warm white, fire-rated) in kitchen ceiling, wired to
existing switch" beats "new kitchen lights".

Suggest exclusions the trader may have forgotten. Common ones by trade:

- **All trades**: making good / redecoration after work; moving furniture;
  hidden defects (rot, asbestos, damaged pipes or cables found once opened
  up); out-of-hours work; permits and building control fees; skip permits.
- **Plumbing / heating**: system flush or powerflush, upgrading the gas
  supply, replacing radiator valves not listed, boxing in pipes.
- **Electrical**: remedial work found during testing, plastering and
  painting after chasing, certification fees if charged separately.
- **Building / renovation**: structural engineer or architect fees,
  planning and building regulations fees, unforeseen ground conditions.
- **Decorating**: filling beyond minor cracks, wallpaper stripping,
  ceilings, woodwork, paint supply.
- **Landscaping**: waste soil removal, buried services or rubble found
  when digging, plants dying after handover if not watered.
- **Cleaning**: biohazards, exterior windows, moving heavy furniture.

Only include exclusions relevant to this job. Five sharp ones beat twenty
generic ones.

## Step 4: Payment terms and legal notes (check locally)

These are prompts for the trader to check, **not legal advice**. Rules
change; tell the trader to confirm with an official source (gov.uk,
Citizens Advice business guidance, their trade body, or their state's
consumer protection or licensing office).

- **Deposits**: keep them proportionate to materials and booking costs.
  Big upfront deposits put customers off and can be challenged as unfair.
  Some US states cap home improvement deposits (e.g. California limits most
  home improvement down payments to 10% or $1,000, whichever is less). Flag
  this and tell US traders to check their state.
- **UK consumers, contract agreed at their home or away from the trader's
  premises**: the customer usually has a 14-day right to cancel, and the
  trader must give them the cancellation information and a model
  cancellation form. If the trader doesn't, the cancellation period can
  extend by up to 12 months. If the customer wants work to start within the
  14 days, get that request in writing (email is fine). If they then cancel,
  they pay a proportionate amount for work done. Urgent repairs the
  customer called you out for are treated differently. Point the trader to
  the Consumer Contracts Regulations guidance on gov.uk or Business
  Companion.
- **US consumers, sale at their home**: the FTC Cooling-Off Rule gives
  buyers 3 business days to cancel many home sales of $25 or more, and the
  seller must give a written cancellation notice. Repairs or maintenance the
  customer asked you to visit for, and genuine emergencies, are generally
  excluded, but add-ons beyond the request are covered. Many states add
  their own home-solicitation rules. Tell the trader to check.
- **Business customers**: state payment terms (e.g. 30 days). In the UK,
  businesses can claim statutory interest and fixed recovery costs on late
  B2B payments; mention this in the terms (see the `payment-chaser` skill).

## Step 5: Quality checklist (run before replying)

- [ ] Labelled clearly as a quote (fixed) or an estimate (with a range or ceiling).
- [ ] Every scope line is specific enough to check off.
- [ ] Exclusions and assumptions cover the likely arguments for this trade.
- [ ] Prices add up: line items = subtotal, tax correct, total correct. Recalculate.
- [ ] VAT/sales tax stated as included, extra, or not charged.
- [ ] Deposit, stages and final payment all have amounts and due dates.
- [ ] Validity date and how to accept are present.
- [ ] Change/extra-work clause is present.
- [ ] No invented prices, specs, certifications or guarantees.
- [ ] Plain English, no jargon the customer won't know (or explained in brackets).

## Output

1. **The quote**, formatted to paste into an email, Word, Google Docs or a
   quoting app.
2. **A short covering message** (under 80 words) to send with it: thanks
   for the visit, the headline price, the validity date, and an invitation
   to ask questions.
3. **Notes for you** (3–5 bullets): gaps marked `[?]`, assumptions you made,
   legal points to check for their location, and an optional upsell they
   could offer as a priced extra.

If the trader asks you to check an existing quote, return the same
checklist with a pass/fail for each line and a corrected version.
