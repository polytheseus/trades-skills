# Chasing late payment

Late payment is the problem small trades businesses complain about most.
The usual mistake is waiting too long and then sending one angry message.
What works is a calm, dated sequence that gets firmer at each step, keeps
the relationship where possible, and leaves a clean paper trail if it ends
up in court.

This skill drafts the messages and the plan. **It isn't legal advice.**
For anything heading to court, or above small-claims limits, tell the user
to check official guidance or speak to a solicitor/attorney, their trade
association, or Citizens Advice (UK).

## Step 1: Get the facts

Ask only for what's missing:

1. **Invoice**: number, date, amount, what it was for, and the agreed
   payment terms (e.g. "due on completion", "14 days", "30 days").
2. **Days overdue**, and what's been sent already (dates and channel).
3. **Customer type**: consumer (homeowner) or business. This decides
   whether statutory interest applies (Step 3).
4. **Any dispute**: has the customer raised a complaint about the work?
   If yes, deal with that first (Step 2).
5. **Relationship**: a regular customer to keep, or a one-off.
6. **Location**: UK (England/Wales, Scotland, NI), US state, or elsewhere.
7. **Payment details**: bank details or a payment link to include.

## Step 2: Is it a dispute or just late?

- **Just late** (no complaint, the customer is silent or "forgot"): run the
  sequence in Step 4.
- **Disputed** (the customer says something's wrong with the work):
  chasing harder makes it worse. Draft a reply that acknowledges the
  specific complaint, offers to inspect or fix if reasonable, and asks for
  payment of any **undisputed** part now. Keep it factual and dated.
- **Partial dispute**: ask for the undisputed amount now and set out a
  plan for the disputed amount.
- **Can't pay** (hardship): offer a written payment plan (Step 5). Getting
  paid in instalments beats a court claim.

## Step 3: Interest and charges (check before you claim)

**UK, business customers (B2B):** under the Late Payment of Commercial
Debts (Interest) Act 1998, a business can claim:

- **Statutory interest** at **8% plus the Bank of England base rate**. Use
  the base rate in force on 30 June (for debts that became overdue
  1 July–31 December) or 31 December (for 1 January–30 June). Look up the
  current rate on the Bank of England website; never guess it.
- **Daily interest** = amount owed × (8% + base rate) ÷ 365, multiplied by
  the number of days overdue. Show the working.
- **Fixed compensation** per invoice: **£40** for debts under £1,000,
  **£70** for £1,000–£9,999.99, and **£100** for £10,000 or more.
- If no payment date was agreed, payment is late 30 days after the
  customer received the invoice or the work was done, whichever is later.
- Source: gov.uk, "Late commercial payments: charging interest and debt
  recovery". Tell the user to check it's current.

**UK, consumer customers:** the statutory scheme does **not** apply to
consumers. Interest or late fees can only be charged if the customer
agreed to them in the contract (e.g. in the quote's terms) and they're
reasonable. Don't add charges that weren't agreed.

**US:** interest and late fees depend on the contract and state law
(usury limits, consumer protection rules). Contractors may also have
mechanic's lien rights, but lien notices have strict, short state
deadlines that often run from the last day of work. If the amount is
significant, tell the user to check their state's lien deadlines now,
before they expire, and consider an attorney.

**Elsewhere:** EU countries have late-payment rules for B2B debts (from
the Late Payment Directive), with local details. Tell the user to check.

## Step 4: The escalation sequence

Default timeline. Adjust it to the payment terms and the relationship.

| Step | When | Tone | Channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Pre-reminder | 3 days before due (optional) | Friendly heads-up | Email/text |
| 1. Friendly reminder | 1–3 days overdue | Assume it's an oversight | Email + text |
| 2. Firm reminder | 7–14 days overdue | Clear, factual, a date to pay by | Email + phone call |
| 3. Final notice | 21–30 days overdue | Firm, states what happens next | Email + post |
| 4. Letter before action | 30–45 days overdue | Formal, 14 days to pay | Post (recorded) + email |
| 5. Next step | After the deadline | Act on what you said | Court claim / agency |

Never threaten something the user isn't prepared to do. Every message
includes: invoice number, date, amount, what it's for, how to pay, and a
specific pay-by date.

### 1. Friendly reminder
Short and warm. "Just a quick reminder that invoice [#] for [job] ([£/$
amount]) was due on [date]. Here are the payment details again: […]. If
you've already paid, thank you, and please ignore this." Attach the invoice
again.

### 2. Firm reminder
"Invoice [#] for [£/$ amount] is now [N] days overdue. Please pay by
[date, 7 days out]. If there's a problem with the invoice or the work,
let me know today so we can sort it out." Suggest the user also phones:
calls get paid faster than emails.

### 3. Final notice
State the history (dates of previous reminders), the amount, and a final
date (7 days). State the next step plainly: "If payment isn't received by
[date], I'll send a formal letter before action and may start a court claim
without further notice." For UK B2B, add the statutory interest and
compensation figures (with working) or say they'll be added.

### 4. Letter before action
A formal letter. In England and Wales, a debt claim against an individual
(including a sole trader) should follow the **Pre-Action Protocol for Debt
Claims**: give the debtor **30 days** to respond, and include an
information sheet and reply form. For claims against a limited company,
14 days is usually reasonable. Tell the user to check the protocol on
justice.gov.uk before sending. Structure:

1. Heading: "LETTER BEFORE ACTION", the date, and the debtor's name and
   address.
2. The debt: invoice(s), dates, work done, amount, and terms agreed.
3. The history: reminders sent and replies received.
4. The total claimed, broken down (principal + interest + compensation
   where applicable).
5. The deadline and payment methods.
6. The consequence: a court claim (in England and Wales, the online Money
   Claims service for claims up to £10,000 in the small claims track), plus
   court fees and further interest. Note that a judgment could affect the
   debtor's credit rating.
7. An invitation to propose a payment plan or tell the user about any
   dispute.

Scotland and Northern Ireland have different court procedures, and so
does each US state (small claims limits vary widely). Tell the user to
check theirs.

### 5. After the deadline
Set out the options with pros and cons: a small claims court claim
(online in many places), a debt collection agency (usually takes a
percentage), stopping further work for that customer, or writing it off.
For US contractors, check the lien deadline first. Don't draft threats to
publish the customer's name or post about them online. That can be
defamatory or count as harassment.

## Step 5: Payment plan option

If the customer can't pay in full, draft a short written agreement: total
owed, instalment amount and dates, payment method, what happens if an
instalment is missed (the full balance becomes due), and whether interest
is frozen while they keep to the plan. Both parties confirm in writing.

## Tone rules

- Polite, factual, dated. No sarcasm, capitals or exclamation marks.
- Don't apologise for asking to be paid.
- Avoid "As per my last email" and "Please be advised". Say what you mean
  in plain words.
- Keep each message short: under 120 words for steps 1–3.
- Never include anything that could be harassment: no repeated late-night
  calls, no contacting the customer's employer, family or neighbours, no
  public shaming.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] It's clear whether this is a dispute or a simple late payment, and the response fits.
- [ ] Invoice number, date, amount, job and payment details are in every message.
- [ ] Every message has a specific pay-by date.
- [ ] Interest/charges are only claimed where the law or the contract allows, and the working is shown.
- [ ] The base rate is marked "check current rate" unless the user supplied it.
- [ ] Each step's consequence is something the user is prepared to do.
- [ ] No legal advice is presented as certain. Location-specific points are flagged for checking.

## Output

1. **Where you are**: one line ("Invoice 14 days overdue, business customer, UK: send step 2 today").
2. **The next message**, ready to send (subject line + body), plus a short SMS/WhatsApp version.
3. **The rest of the sequence** with dates filled in, so the user can schedule it.
4. **Interest calculation** if applicable, with working.
5. **Notes**: anything to check locally, and how to prevent this next time
   (deposit, stage payments, shorter terms, payment link on the invoice,
   late-payment terms in the quote).
